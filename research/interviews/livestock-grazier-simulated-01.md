**Invented context**: "Dave", specialist beef breeder-backgrounder,
~4,000ha of mixed grazing country near Roma, southern Queensland. Runs a
self-replacing Brahman-cross breeding herd, sells weaners and some
backgrounded stock. Family operation — Dave, his wife Kerry (does the
books and half the mustering), one part-time station hand, contract
musterers and a contract shearer for the small sheep flock they still run
on the side. Interview is set in a year following a run of below-average
rainfall, i.e. deliberately not a "good year" scenario, since the persona
file flags income volatility as a defining feature to test.

---

## Walk through yesterday

**Interviewer**: Talk me through yesterday, start to finish.

**Dave**: Up before six, checked the two closest waters on the way to feed
the poddies, then took the ute round the back paddocks — we've got about
1,800 head of breeders split across six paddocks at the moment, so it's a
rotation, not every paddock every day. Spent a fair bit of time in the
Gidgee paddock because I'd noticed a few days back the cows in there were
looking tucked up compared to the mob in Number 9, and I wanted to see if
that was still the case or whether I was imagining it. Rang Kerry's
brother, who's our agent, around ten to ask what weaner prices were doing
at the last Roma sale, because we've got about 200 coming ready in the
next month and I'm trying to work out whether to sell store now or hang on
and background them a bit longer if the season allows. Afternoon was
fixing a bore pump that had dropped out overnight — that ate about three
hours I didn't have. Called it a day around six, wrote nothing down
anywhere except a text to Kerry about the pump part I need to order.

## Plan

**Interviewer**: How far ahead do you plan?

**Dave**: Joining and weaning dates are set a year out, pretty much fixed
around when we want calves hitting the ground relative to the season break.
Everything else — stocking rate, whether we background or sell store, when
we muster — that's all set inside the season, not before it. I couldn't
tell you in January what we'll be doing in August. Depends on rain.

**Interviewer**: What's actually written down versus in your head?

**Dave**: Joining dates are on the calendar in the shed. NLIS movements are
recorded because you have to. Everything else — paddock rotations, who's
looking light, what the plan is for the next fortnight — that's in my
head, or it's a conversation with Kerry over dinner.

## Monitor conditions

**Interviewer**: What do you actually look at on a check?

**Dave**: Body condition first — are they carrying themselves the way
they should for the time of year. Then water — is the trough full, is the
float working, any sign of a leak. Then the fence line if I'm going past
it anyway. Pasture — how much is actually there versus how much I think is
there, which honestly I get wrong more than I'd like to admit, especially
this time of year when it's patchy.

**Interviewer**: How often do you get to every paddock?

**Dave**: The close paddocks, most days. The back country, maybe once a
week unless there's a reason to be out there. That's the honest answer —
horticulture blokes probably walk their whole property every day; we
physically can't with the country we've got.

## Make production decisions

**Interviewer**: What was the last thing you decided on your own, without
calling anyone?

**Dave**: Moving the Gidgee mob into Number 9 three days ago, once I was
sure they were actually behind where they should be, not just my
imagination. That's just experience — you know what that mob normally
looks like this time of year.

**Interviewer**: What was the last time you called someone about
something you noticed?

**Dave**: Rang the vet about a couple of calves scouring last month.
That's not a call-my-neighbour thing, that's a "is this something that's
going to go through the whole mob" thing.

## Trust — who's the trusted voice?

**Interviewer**: If you had to name one person whose advice you trust most
on this property, who is it?

**Dave**: That's actually a hard one, and it's not one person the way I
think a horticulture bloke might say "my agronomist." For animal health
it's the vet, but she's an hour and a half away and I only really call her
for something that looks serious. For anything to do with selling — timing,
who's buying, what the market's doing — that's Kerry's brother, our agent,
and I trust him more than any report I could read myself, because he
actually knows our cattle and he's not going to steer us wrong on a sale
because he wants the next one too. For the general "how should we be
running this place" stuff — pasture management, herd genetics, that kind
of thing — that's more the field days and the odd workshop, BeefUp and
that sort of thing. I've picked up good ideas there, but nobody from those
programs has ever set foot on this place and told me what to do with this
paddock specifically.

**Interviewer**: So there isn't a single person who plays that "knows my
whole operation" role?

**Dave**: No, and now that you ask it straight out — no, there isn't. The
agent's closest to it because he's the one I ring the most and he does
know the property. But he's not out here walking it with me, he's working
off what I tell him plus what he sees at the yards.

## Procure inputs

**Interviewer**: How do you decide what to buy and from whom?

**Dave**: Supplements and lick, mostly the same supplier every year unless
price gets silly. The one that actually worries me is hay in a dry run —
last year I was ringing five different contacts trying to find anything
within cartage distance, and by the time I found some the price had moved
against me twice. That's the one where I'd pay for better information if
someone could actually tell me what's around and what it's going for,
instead of me working the phone.

## Manage labour/equipment

**Interviewer**: How do you handle equipment failure?

**Dave**: Yesterday's bore pump is a good example — three hours gone, and
that's a good outcome, because I had the part. If I hadn't, that's a whole
day and a truck trip to town. We don't have anyone on retainer, it's
whoever can come fastest.

## Harvest (muster/weaning/sale prep)

**Interviewer**: How do you decide when to muster/wean?

**Dave**: Weaning's roughly fixed to the calendar, give or take two weeks
either way for season. Mustering for sale prep is more fluid — depends on
contractor availability as much as anything. We've had to push a muster
back because the contract crew was booked on someone else's place.

## Sell/output

**Interviewer**: Walk me through the last sale timing decision.

**Dave**: Right now, actually — the 200 weaners I mentioned. Agent reckons
the market's soft at Roma right now but there's talk it firms in six weeks.
Problem is I don't know if I've got six weeks of feed for them if I hold.
So it's not really a market decision on its own, it's a market decision
tangled up with a feed decision, and I'm juggling both in my head with
half the information I'd want on either one.

**Interviewer**: What would make you more confident?

**Dave**: Something that actually put the feed question and the price
question next to each other instead of me holding one in my head while
I'm on the phone about the other. I check saleyard reports myself, but by
the time I've read three of them I've lost the thread on what our own
paddock can actually carry for six more weeks.

## Manage finance/compliance

**Interviewer**: How do you use your NLIS records day to day?

**Dave**: Honestly, not at all beyond what I have to log for the movement.
It's compliance, not a tool. Kerry does the books separately in a
spreadsheet that's really about cash in, cash out, not connected to the
NLIS side of things at all.

## Review & plan next season

**Interviewer**: How do you assess whether the season went well?

**Dave**: Weaning percentage, and what we got per head compared to last
year, roughly, from memory. We don't sit down and compare it to a plan
because there wasn't a written plan to compare it to — more a "did that
feel like a good year or a hard year."

## Software tried and dropped

**Interviewer**: Any grazing/livestock software you've tried?

**Dave**: Kerry had a go at one of the grazing chart apps a couple of
years back — put a season's worth of rainfall and mob movements into it.
Fell off after about four months, mostly because it needed more typing in
than either of us had the patience for after a full day, not because it
was wrong about anything. If it could've worked off what we already
naturally talk about or write in a text, we might've stuck with it.

## Companion: the agent's perspective (simulated)

**Interviewer**: What's the most common reason a grazier doesn't act on
your advice?

**Agent (simulated)**: It's rarely that they disagree with me. It's more
that by the time they're ready to act, the window's moved, or something
else on the property's eaten their attention — a pump, a fence, whatever's
in front of them that day. I'd say the biggest thing I wish I had is a
better read on their actual feed position when we're talking sale timing,
because right now I'm going off what they tell me on the phone, and
they're not always sure themselves.

## What this transcript suggests for the framework (to test for real)

1. **Supports** the "distributed advisor, not a single agronomist
   equivalent" hypothesis — but with the **stock agent emerging as the
   closest single analogue specifically for the sale-timing decision**,
   which sharpens (doesn't just confirm) the opportunity card in
   `opportunities/livestock-sale-timing-advisor.md`: the AI intervention
   there should probably be framed as extending the agent relationship
   specifically, not a generic "advisor," and the agent's own stated gap
   (poor visibility into the grazier's actual feed position) is a
   concrete, testable design input for that card.
2. **New friction point not yet in the journey file**: the sale-timing
   decision is entangled with a feed-availability decision that the
   grazier is holding in his head simultaneously — the journey file
   currently lists these as adjacent but separate friction points
   (Procure inputs / Sell-output). A real interview should test whether
   they're actually one combined decision moment, which would change how
   the sale-timing opportunity card should be scoped.
3. **Corroborates** (doesn't prove) the persona's rejection-trigger
   hypothesis about "another platform to log into" — the invented software
   dropout story (Kerry's grazing chart app) is consistent with, but
   doesn't independently validate, the existing `[VALIDATE]` tag on that
   point.
4. **New hypothesis**: NLIS data is pure compliance, completely
   disconnected from the grower's own financial tracking (a separate
   spreadsheet) — if true in reality, this means the "already-structured
   compliance data" this persona has (unlike horticulture) may be less
   useful as an AI opportunity input than first assumed, since it isn't
   even linked to the farm's own record-keeping today.
5. Feed-procurement uncertainty in a dry run ("ringing five different
   contacts") is a sharper, more specific version of the journey file's
   `[VALIDATE]`-tagged Procure Inputs row — worth turning into its own
   opportunity card once validated.

None of the above should be treated as settled — they're now specific
enough to walk into a real interview and either confirm or overturn, which
was the point of running this simulation.
