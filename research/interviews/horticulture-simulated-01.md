**Invented context**: "Maria", second-generation grower on ~220ha near
Robinvale (Sunraysia), running almonds on the majority of the property
plus a smaller citrus block — deliberately reflecting the ABARES
multi-crop finding logged in `research/sources.md` rather than a
single-commodity grower. Owner-operator with her husband Tony, one
full-time employee, seasonal labour at harvest, a contracted agronomist,
and almonds sold through a processor/handler rather than direct to a
packing shed. Interview set mid-season, after a run of a few warmer-than-
usual weeks.

---

## Walk through yesterday

**Interviewer**: Talk me through yesterday, start to finish.

**Maria**: Up around 5:30, out in the almonds before it got hot — walked
blocks 3 and 4 first because they're the ones I've been keeping an eye on,
checking hull split progress and just generally how the canopy's looking.
Noticed block 4's a bit patchier than block 3, which is unusual because
they're the same variety and age, so I took a few photos on my phone,
more for my own memory than anything. Rang our irrigation contractor
around eight about a dripper line that looked like it was underperforming
in the corner of block 4 — turned out that's probably why that corner's
behind, not disease, which was a relief. Spent the rest of the morning on
the citrus block doing a general check, that one doesn't get the same
attention because it's smaller and it's honestly more Tony's project.
Afternoon was two hours of paperwork — chemical use records, because we
had a QA audit coming up — and a call with our agronomist about the
nutrition program, which was actually a scheduled call, not a reactive
one. Finished the day not really sure if block 4 was fixed or if I need to
go back and look again in a few days.

## Plan

**Interviewer**: How far ahead do you plan, and what's written down?

**Maria**: The nutrition and spray program for the whole season comes from
our agronomist, usually set in about July for the season ahead, and that
is written down — he sends it through as a document, a proper program with
dates and products. Irrigation is more responsive to what's actually
happening, so that's not planned out the same way. Big decisions — like
when we replanted block 2 four years ago — that's a multi-year thing,
bank's involved, agronomist's involved, that's genuinely planned.
Day-to-day, week-to-week stuff is just me and Tony deciding as we go.

## Monitor conditions

**Interviewer**: What do you check, and how much of it is written down
anywhere?

**Maria**: Canopy, fruit/hull condition, obvious pest or disease signs,
irrigation lines. Almost none of it is written down in any structured
way. I take photos on my phone reasonably often, more than I used to,
because with four kids and a lot going on I don't trust my memory for
"was that the same three weeks ago." But those photos just sit in my
camera roll — I don't do anything with them unless something's actually
gone wrong and I want to show the agronomist.

**Interviewer**: How many separate information sources do you actually
use in a normal week?

**Maria**: More than I'd like. Weather app for the forecast, another app
the almond board pushes out with regional pest alerts, my own eyes on the
walk, and then whatever the agronomist tells me on the phone or at a
visit. They don't talk to each other. If the pest alert app says
something's active in the region, I've got to remember to actually go
check for it on the walk — it doesn't prompt me at the right moment.

## Make production decisions

**Interviewer**: What was the last thing you decided on your own?

**Maria**: The dripper line yesterday — well, that was really the
contractor's call once I flagged it, but deciding it was worth ringing
about rather than waiting was mine. And two weeks ago I decided to bring
harvest prep forward slightly on block 3 because it looked ahead of block
4 — that's the kind of call where I'm using my own read of "this looks
like it's tracking early" against nothing more solid than experience.

**Interviewer**: What's the last piece of agronomist advice you didn't
follow through on properly?

**Maria**: [long pause] Probably a soil amendment he recommended for
block 4 back at the start of the season — it's on the program, but with
everything else going on I don't think we actually got it done in the
window he wanted. Nobody's fault exactly, it just didn't have a trigger
that made it urgent on any particular day, so it kept sliding.

## Trust

**Interviewer**: What would make you trust a system telling you something
about a block, without seeing it yourself?

**Maria**: It'd have to know that block, specifically — not "almonds in
this region typically." Block 4 has always been our trickiest one, it's
the one with a bit of salinity in the corner, and any generic advice that
doesn't know that history isn't going to get taken seriously. And it'd
need to be able to say why, the same way [agronomist] does when he walks
the block — "this looks like X because of Y" — not just a score.

**Interviewer**: Tell me about a piece of technology you tried and
stopped using.

**Maria**: We had a soil moisture probe system in for two seasons. The
data was fine, but it lived in its own app that nobody checked unless
something had already gone wrong, and setting the alert thresholds
properly took more time than either of us had to give it. It's still out
there, I think half the batteries are dead now.

## Procure inputs

**Interviewer**: How do you decide what to buy and from whom?

**Maria**: Mostly the same suppliers year to year for chemicals and
nutrition products, driven by the agronomist's program more than price
shopping — he specifies the product, we source it, usually from whoever's
closest or has a standing account with us. Water is the one where there's
real decision-making — how much to buy on the temporary market versus
rely on allocation, and that's as much a finance conversation with our
accountant as it is a farming one.

## Manage labour/equipment

**Interviewer**: How do you handle labour, especially at harvest?

**Maria**: Our one full-time bloke plus a harvest crew we've used for
years, booked months ahead. The hard part isn't finding them, it's timing
the booking against a harvest date we can't actually nail down until much
closer to the time.

## Harvest

**Interviewer**: How do you decide when to start?

**Maria**: Hull split percentage, mostly, plus what the processor's
telling us about their intake schedule — we're not the only ones
delivering to them, so there's a bit of a queue to think about, not just
our own readiness.

## Sell/output

**Interviewer**: How much visibility do you have into price at point of
sale?

**Maria**: Almonds go to the processor under a pooling arrangement, so I
don't have day-to-day price visibility the way I imagine a livestock
person watching saleyard reports would — the price comes out later based
on the pool result. Citrus is a bit more immediate because that goes
through a packer on spec and price. So actually the two crops on the same
property have quite different "do I have market visibility" answers.

## Manage finance/compliance

**Interviewer**: How do you keep the records compliance requires?

**Maria**: Chemical use records are kept properly — we have to, for the
QA scheme, and I do them within a day or two of application, on a
template our agronomist gave us years ago. It's not digital in any
connected sense, it's a spreadsheet that lives on one laptop. It does
occasionally help — when something's gone wrong I've gone back through it
to check what we sprayed and when — but that's a happy side effect, not
why we keep it.

## Review & plan next season

**Interviewer**: How do you assess whether the season went well?

**Maria**: Yield per block against last year, and against what the
agronomist expected going in. That comparison happens, but it's a
conversation at the end-of-season visit, not something either of us has
prepared for with real data — it's "how do you reckon block 4 went" more
than pulling up a report.
